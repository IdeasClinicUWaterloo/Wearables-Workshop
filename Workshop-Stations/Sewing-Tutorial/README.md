# Sewing Basics: Creating a Storage Pouch

![Finished quilted owl-print pouch with a flap and translucent button closure, held in a hand](./images/image9.png)

Machine sewing is a valuable skill for creating wearable engineering projects. In this tutorial, you will learn how to safely operate a sewing machine, select appropriate fabrics, measure and cut materials, and assemble simple fabric components. You will do this through learning how to sew a glasses case (or a phone pouch if you do not want to make a glasses case). These foundational techniques will help you create durable, comfortable wearables that can securely support prototypes and engineering devices.

## Selecting Fabric

The first step in any sewing project is selecting the appropriate fabric. Fabrics differ in their weight, stretch, durability, and ease of sewing, all of which affect the performance of your final design.

For this workshop, we will be using a **medium-weight woven cotton**. Compared to knit fabrics, woven fabrics have little to no stretch, making them easier to measure, cut, pin, and sew. This stability makes them a good choice for beginners and for projects that require a consistent shape.

| Fabric Type | Characteristics | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| *Woven (e.g., cotton, canvas, denim)* | Little to no stretch, holds its shape, easier to sew | Beginners, structured wearables, device mounting |
| *Knit (e.g., jersey, spandex, athletic fabrics)* | Stretchy, flexible, conforms to the body, can shift while sewing | Form-fitting garments, projects requiring built-in stretch |

![Diagram comparing woven fabric structure (threads crossing over and under each other) with knitted fabric structure (interlocking loops)](./images/image2.png)

Fabric weight is another important consideration:

| Fabric Weight | Characteristics | Typical Uses |
|---|---|---|
| *Lightweight* | Thin, breathable, less durable | Linings, lightweight clothing |
| *Medium-weight* | Balanced strength and flexibility | Most wearable prototypes and general sewing projects |
| *Heavyweight* | Thick, durable, more difficult to sew | Bags, heavy-duty straps, projects supporting larger loads |

Although woven cotton has limited stretch, flexibility can still be added to a design by incorporating elastic straps or bands where movement is needed. This provides a practical way to improve comfort while maintaining the stability of the fabric.

## Creating the Template

This project also uses batting, a soft layer of padding placed between two layers of fabric. The batting will be sandwiched between the two fabric layers before sewing. Batting adds cushioning, making wearable devices more comfortable against the body while also helping the fabric maintain its shape.

Before cutting your fabric, create one paper template. This template will be reused to cut all of the pattern pieces, so you only need to measure and draw it once. To create the different pieces, simply fold the template as instructed before pinning the template in place and cutting.

**Step 1: Measure Your Item**

- Measure the length and width of the item you would like to store (in this tutorial, a pair of glasses).
- Measure the longest point for the length.
- Measure the widest point for the width.
- Since your glasses are three-dimensional, measure over the glasses from one side to the other rather than measuring straight across. This ensures there is enough room for the glasses to fit comfortably inside the case.

**Step 2: Add a Seam Allowance**

- Add a 1 cm seam allowance to all sides of your measurements.
- A seam allowance is the extra fabric around the edge that will be sewn together. This is to allow your project to not get smaller once it's finished.

**Step 3: Draw the Template**

- Using your measurements, draw the paper template.
- Draw a rectangle using the finished dimensions plus the seam allowance.
- Create the curved flap by drawing a semicircle with a radius equal to half of the width.
- Cut out the paper template along the outside edge.

Your paper template is now ready to be used for cutting all of the fabric and batting pieces.

## Cutting the Fabric

Before cutting, press your cotton fabric using an iron set to high heat with steam. Pressing removes wrinkles and helps ensure your pattern pieces are cut accurately. For the batting, you can skip ironing or iron the polyester batting with cotton fabric over it on medium heat and steam.

![Purple owl-print and solid lilac fabric pieces being pressed with an iron on a cutting mat](./images/image10.png)

To cut the **back pieces**, pin the paper template as is to the outer fabric and cut around them as closely as possible using fabric scissors. Repeat this for the inner lining. You should have 2 back pieces when you are done cutting.

![Paper graph-paper template pinned onto owl-print fabric along a rectangular cutting line, folded to mark the back piece shape](./images/image14.png)

To cut the **front pieces**, fold the template along the green line and pin to the outer fabric. Repeat the steps for the back pieces. You should have 2 front pieces following this. To cut the **batting back piece**, fold the template along the red dotted line (including the semicircle above). Pin and cut as previously. For the **batting front piece**, fold along the red dotted line parallel to the green line. You should have a rectangular shape. Again, pin and cut. You should now have 2 back pieces, 2 front pieces, and 2 pieces of batting. Iron the cut pieces once again before sewing.

![Paper template with a rounded top pinned onto a folded layer of white batting, ready to be cut](./images/image8.png)

## Threading the Machine

Before you start sewing, you must make sure your sewing machine is threaded. First, ensure the presser foot is raised and the needle is in its highest position. This allows the thread to pass correctly through the machine's tension discs.

> **Note:** Threading paths vary slightly between sewing machines. Follow along with a station helper or refer to the machine's threading diagram while completing this step. The accompanying video demonstrates the complete threading process for the machine used in this workshop.

📺 [Watch: Threading the machine](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q8xr-iW54Ls)

1. Place the spool of thread onto the spool pin and secure it with the spool cap.
2. Follow the numbered thread guides on the machine, making sure the thread passes through the tension discs and the take-up lever.
3. Thread the needle from front to back.
4. Insert a wound bobbin into the bobbin compartment following the machine's markings.
5. Hold the upper thread and rotate the handwheel toward you one full turn to bring the bobbin thread to the surface.
6. Pull both the upper and bobbin threads underneath the presser foot and toward the back of the machine, leaving approximately 10–15 cm (4–6 in) of thread before beginning to sew.

## Pinning and Sewing the Front and Back Pieces

With the right sides of the fabric facing each other, align one front piece with the other front piece. Put the batting on top of the wrong side of either fabric. Secure the edges using sewing pins or clips, placing them perpendicular to the edge of the fabric.

![Two batting pieces, a rectangular back piece and a rounded front piece, pinned in place with colorful pins](./images/image15.png)

Repeat this process for both the outer fabric and the inner lining. Ensure your machine is set to straight stitching with medium tension and length. Be sure to test the stitches on a scrap piece of fabric before sewing your project. Begin sewing at the edge of your batting, ensuring it is also being stitched.

Remember to backstitch at the beginning and end of each seam. Backstitching locks the stitches in place and prevents them from coming undone. You can backstitch by following the diagram below.

![Hand-drawn diagram of a curved pouch flap showing where to start and finish sewing the curved seam, with instructions to stop, lift the presser foot, and turn the project 180 degrees](./images/image22.png)

**Important:** Leave the bottom edge of each side open. This opening will be used later to turn the pouch right-side out. Sew slowly around the curved flap, removing pins as you approach them. Do not sew over pins, as this can damage the needle or sewing machine.

## Trimming the Seam Allowance

Once the pieces have been sewn together, trim the seam allowance to reduce bulk.

If available, use pinking shears to trim the curved edges. Unlike regular scissors, pinking shears create a zigzag edge that helps reduce fabric fraying while making curved seams smoother once turned right-side out. If you do not have pinking shears, carefully cut small notches into the curved seam allowance without cutting through the stitches. These notches will allow for smoothness along a curve.

![Two sewn pouch pieces with rounded flaps in front of a sewing machine, trimmed with a zigzag pinked edge](./images/image20.png)

Turn both the outer fabric and lining right-side out through the opening left along the bottom edge, ensuring that you are turning them out such that the batting is inside and the right sides of the fabric are on the outside. You can use a blunt tool, such as the end of a pencil or chopstick, to gently push out the rounded flap.

![Hand turning a small pouch piece right-side out, showing the owl-print fabric emerging through the opening](./images/image16.png)

Press both pieces flat with an iron before continuing.

![Two flat pressed pouch pieces side by side, one solid lilac and one owl-print, both with rounded tops](./images/image18.png)

## Adding Quilting Lines

Once the two sides have been pressed and turned inside out, we can add quilting lines to improve the appearance of the pouch and create channels that can be used to route wires in future wearable electronics projects.

Using a ruler and tailor's chalk, measure and mark vertical lines 1 cm apart across the pouch. Ensure the lines are straight and evenly spaced. Sew along each marked line using the same machine settings used previously. Backstitching is not necessary, as these quilting lines are decorative and are only used to hold the layers together and create wire channels.

If incorporating electronics into your design, these quilted channels can help organize and route wires neatly through the fabric. Components can also be secured by hand using embroidery thread to the batting before sewing it down.

> **Important:** Avoid sewing over electronic components or wires with a sewing machine.

![Three-step sequence showing a ruler and bar of soap used to mark evenly spaced vertical lines on owl-print fabric, ending with the fully marked pouch piece](./images/image7.png)

## Closing the Bottom Edge

If you have not already done so, trim the open edge using pinking shears. The zigzag edge helps reduce fraying while you work with the fabric.

Using the same machine settings as before, sew a straight line along the open edge. This line of stitching helps stabilize the fabric and makes it easier to fold the edge neatly.

Next, fold the edge over as close to the stitched line as possible and press it flat with an iron. Sew another straight line along the folded edge to secure the hem.

Repeat these steps for both the outer pouch and the inner lining.

![Before-and-after comparison of the pouch's bottom edge: pinked zigzag edge with quilting lines on the left, folded and hemmed edge on the right](./images/image13.png)

## Sewing Both Pieces Together

Place the back piece and front piece on top of each other such that the right sides of the fabric are facing each other. Align the side seams and top edges, then secure them with pins or clips.

Using the same machine settings as before, sew around the sides and bottom of the pouch using approximately a 0.5 cm seam allowance. Backstitch at the beginning and end of the seam to secure it.

Turn the entire pouch right-side out by pulling it through the opening. Press the pouch with an iron.

## Creating and Reinforcing the Buttonhole

Close the flap and determine where the buttonhole should be placed. Using your button as a guide, measure and mark a slit about the length of the button's diameter using tailor's chalk.

![Hand holding a translucent star-patterned button up against the pouch flap to mark where the buttonhole should go](./images/image12.png)

Carefully cut along the marked line using embroidery scissors.

![Hand holding open a small cut slit in the quilted flap fabric, revealing the batting underneath](./images/image17.png)

Embroidery thread consists of six strands. Separate three strands and thread them through your embroidery needle. Using a blanket stitch, reinforce the raw edges of the buttonhole to prevent fraying. This technique can also be used to reinforce openings ("windows") in future wearable engineering projects where wires, sensors, or displays pass through the fabric.

![Purple embroidery thread and scissors, showing a loose length of thread with frayed ends](./images/image3.png)

📺 [Watch: Blanket stitch technique](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ofnBmni1k4o)

![Close-up of the rounded flap of the pouch with a buttonhole slit reinforced with purple blanket stitching](./images/image4.png)

## Attaching the Button

Close the flap and align the completed buttonhole with the front of the pouch. Mark the button location using tailor's chalk. Thread your needle with three strands of embroidery thread and securely sew the button in place. Tie a knot on the inside of the pouch and trim any excess thread.

📺 [Watch: Sewing on the button](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AqUaOIXmdCQ)

Test the closure by fastening the button through the buttonhole. The button should fit snugly while still opening and closing easily.

![Finished flat pouch with the flap open, showing the translucent star button sewn onto the front through the reinforced buttonhole](./images/image5.png)

Congratulations! You have now finished your first sewing project 🙂
